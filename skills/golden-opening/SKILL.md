# Golden opening

Use this Skill when the work is specifically about a novel's first scene, first chapter, or first three chapters. It supports planning, drafting, diagnosis, and revision. It does not replace a full outline, chapter continuation, market research, or a general prose-polish pass unless the user asks to apply that work to the opening.

The goal is a readable opening that makes a concrete promise, puts pressure on a character who can act, proves the story's distinctive appeal early, and ends each chapter with an unfinished consequence. Treat all numbers below as diagnostic heuristics, not universal laws; genre, platform, audience, and the user's chosen pace take priority.

## Choose the work mode

Infer the mode from the request:

- **Plan**: create an opening contract and a beat map for one to three chapters.
- **Draft**: write the requested opening prose from the available story material.
- **Diagnose**: explain why an existing opening loses attention and give evidence-based fixes.
- **Revise**: preserve the user's premise, voice, and canon while repairing the highest-impact opening problems.
- **Hybrid**: when the user asks for “优化/重写”, diagnose briefly, then deliver the revised prose; do not stop at a report.

If key story details are missing, make the smallest reasonable assumptions and label them in the planning notes. Do not invent a large setting bible before the opening can move.

## Build the opening contract first

Before drafting or making a major revision, state internally (or show it only when the user asks for a plan):

1. **Reader promise**: the experience the title, premise, and opening jointly promise (for example, revenge, investigation, romance under pressure, progression, or survival).
2. **Protagonist entry**: who the reader meets, what they want now, what blocks them, and what a failure costs.
3. **Distinctive mechanism**: the specific relationship, ability, rule, profession, setting, or dilemma that makes this story worth following.
4. **First proof**: the earliest concrete event that demonstrates the promise instead of merely describing it.
5. **Three chapter jobs**: what changes in Chapters 1, 2, and 3, and the question or unfinished action each chapter hands forward.

Check that the book title, synopsis, and opening make compatible promises. If they conflict, identify the conflict before polishing sentences.

## Route the opening shape

Select the shape that best serves the story's promise. Do not force every genre into a crisis opening.

| Shape | Enter through | Best when |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Crisis | an immediate threat, loss, deadline, or accusation | survival, progression, revenge, high-pressure romance |
| Mystery | an abnormal fact, missing person, impossible message, or contradiction | suspense, investigation, identity, speculative fiction |
| Reversal | a familiar situation whose power relationship or meaning flips | rebirth, transmigration, hidden identity, social drama |
| Relationship collision | a charged request, betrayal, reunion, or forced bargain | romance, family, workplace, character-led fiction |
| Event | a concrete task, competition, ceremony, job, or public decision | sports, industry, historical, ensemble, light comedy |

The shape is an entry point, not a formula. Keep the reader's expected emotional payoff visible even when the opening is quiet or literary.

## Plan the first three chapters

Use the following functions as a flexible spine. Replace any function that would damage the chosen genre or an explicitly requested slow burn.

### Chapter 1 — make the reader take a side

- Enter at the first consequential moment, not at routine preparation.
- Show one protagonist trait through a choice, action, or cost; do not list traits.
- Establish the immediate pressure, a near-term goal, and what failure changes.
- Deliver the first recognizable proof of the story's distinctive mechanism or emotional promise.
- End on a hard turn: an interrupted action, new evidence, an arrival, a decision, or a consequence already in motion.

### Chapter 2 — make the premise operate

- Escalate the original problem or expose the cost of solving it.
- Put the protagonist's special mechanism, relationship, or skill into a concrete situation.
- Reveal only the setting information needed to understand the current choice.
- Give a small payoff, reversal, or new advantage, then attach a larger complication.
- End with a question whose answer requires the next chapter, rather than a vague promise that things will get exciting.

### Chapter 3 — set the serial contract

- Confirm the story's operating mode: what kinds of conflict and satisfaction the reader should expect.
- Complete a first local objective or visible win where the genre needs one; a mystery may instead provide a decisive clue.
- Open the medium-term direction: an enemy, investigation, mission, relationship bargain, destination, or escalating rule.
- Leave one durable thread that can pull the reader beyond the opening arc.

The three chapters should form a rising chain: **pressure → operation → direction**. A chapter may contain relief, humor, tenderness, or quiet observation, but the scene must still change a relationship, risk, resource, belief, or plan.

## Drafting constraints

Apply these as checks, not as rigid bans:

- The first paragraph should contain a concrete situation, an active character, and a reason the moment matters. Avoid default weather, waking, commuting, or panoramic scenery unless the abnormality is the hook.
- By roughly the first 100–300 Chinese characters (or the equivalent opening screen), let the reader see at least one of: conflict, abnormality, unfairness, danger, a meaningful desire, or the distinctive mechanism.
- Keep early named-character load low. Introduce a new person only when they change the current pressure or information balance.
- Put worldbuilding inside action, dialogue, objects, and consequences. Delay encyclopedic history and terminology until the reader needs it.
- Prefer physical behavior, specific choices, and sensory evidence over repeated labels such as “he felt”, “she realized”, or “the atmosphere was tense”.
- Keep the protagonist's goal legible. A fight, escape, meeting, or investigation should answer “what are they trying to change right now?”
- Do not claim a payoff that the first three chapters cannot demonstrate. If the title promises a system, profession, romance, revenge, or mystery, show its first usable evidence early.
- End on an event or decision in progress. Avoid a summary, moral, generic foreshadowing, or a rhetorical “what will happen next?” question.

## Hook quality

Classify each chapter-end hook by what remains unresolved:

- **Scene hook**: an immediate action or danger is interrupted.
- **Information hook**: a clue, identity, rule, or contradiction changes the reader's model.
- **Choice hook**: the protagonist commits to a costly action.
- **Consequence hook**: the prior choice produces a new threat, debt, witness, or opportunity.

Prefer a specific unfinished event over a narrator's promise. A strong opening normally varies hook types across the three chapters so the reader is pulled by both immediate tension and a larger direction.

## Diagnose before rewriting

When reviewing existing prose, locate the first point where a reader can answer all four questions: **Who is here? What do they want now? What blocks them? Why does this matter?** Then inspect what happens before that point.

Use these failure categories:

| Failure | Evidence to look for | Smallest useful repair |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Slow entry | routine, scenery, or history before a consequential choice | cut or relocate setup; enter at the first changed situation |
| Soft conflict | the problem is named but creates no cost or decision | attach a deadline, loss, witness, or opposing action |
| Weak protagonist | traits are explained and the character only reacts | give the protagonist a specific choice with a price |
| Buried premise | the advertised mechanism is absent or only described | stage a small, observable proof in Chapter 1 or 2 |
| Information overload | many names, terms, factions, or rules arrive together | keep only what changes the current scene; defer the rest |
| Flat escalation | each chapter repeats the same pressure | change the risk, information, relationship, or available option |
| Soft ending | the chapter closes with summary or generic anticipation | cut at a concrete reveal, arrival, decision, or consequence |
| Artificial prose | template transitions, stacked abstractions, uniform reactions | replace with specific behavior, uneven sentence rhythm, and character-shaped language |

Do not rewrite the whole opening when one local change fixes the reader's orientation or the first hook. Preserve effective voice, canon, and distinctive images.

## Review gates for a draft

Run a short self-review before returning prose:

1. **Opening screen**: Is there a concrete event or abnormality before the setup becomes explanatory?
2. **Orientation**: Can a new reader identify the protagonist, immediate situation, and near-term pressure without a glossary?
3. **Promise**: Does the first visible proof match the title, synopsis, genre, and requested tone?
4. **Agency**: Does the protagonist make or prepare a meaningful choice rather than only receive information?
5. **Escalation**: Does each chapter alter at least one of risk, knowledge, relationship, resource, or plan?
6. **Hook**: Is every chapter ending anchored to a specific unfinished event, clue, choice, or consequence?
7. **Continuity**: Do character facts, world rules, timeline, and existing workspace material remain consistent?
8. **Voice**: Does the prose sound like this story and character, rather than a generic “high-tension” template?

When the user asks for a score, score dimensions separately (entry, protagonist, promise, escalation, hook, and prose) and cite scene evidence. A score is a decision aid, not evidence of market performance.

## Output rules

- For **plan**, return the opening contract, a chapter-by-chapter beat map, hook type, and the assumptions that need the user's decision.
- For **diagnose**, return the highest-impact problems with quoted short locations or paragraph references, the reason each harms reading, and a prioritized repair plan. Do not silently rewrite unless asked.
- For **draft**, return the requested prose first. Add only the concise planning or continuity notes needed to make the result usable.
- For **revise**, preserve the user's premise and effective passages; explain only material changes if the user asks for rationale.
- If the user requests multiple alternative openings, vary the opening shape or emotional entry point while keeping the same premise, protagonist, and reader promise so the alternatives are comparable.

Do not expose hidden chain-of-thought or claim that a formula guarantees retention, signing, ranking, or commercial success. Distinguish observed text facts, reasonable craft inferences, and choices made for the user's story.
